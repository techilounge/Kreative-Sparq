## 2. Voice rules

### The voice

Kreative Sparq sounds confident, clear, observant, warm, and commercially aware. The writing should feel like a capable person explaining the work across a table. It should never sound like a pitch deck assembled from popular marketing phrases.

### Writing rules

- Lead with the buyer's problem or desired outcome.
- Prefer specific nouns and active verbs.
- Mix short sentences with longer explanatory ones.
- Use contractions where they sound natural.
- Make claims at the level the available proof can support.
- Explain what the agency does, how it works, and what the client receives.
- Name trade-offs honestly. Not every channel, campaign, or deliverable is right for every business.
- Use search phrases only where they fit the meaning of the sentence.
- Let useful detail create authority. Do not announce expertise.

### Avoid these patterns

- “In today's fast-paced digital landscape”
- “Unlock your brand's potential”
- “Elevate your brand”
- “Take your business to the next level”
- “Cutting-edge solutions”
- “Seamless experiences”
- “Tailored solutions for every need”
- “We are passionate about...”
- “Your trusted partner” without proof
- “Leading,” “best,” “number one,” or “world-class” without independent evidence
- “Not just X, but Y” constructions
- Repeated three-item lists used for rhythm rather than meaning
- Sycophantic or over-friendly openings
- Abstract nouns stacked together, such as “innovation, transformation, and excellence”
- Unnecessary words such as “delve,” “leverage,” “utilize,” “foster,” “tapestry,” “realm,” “ecosystem,” “synergy,” “pivotal,” and “multifaceted”

### Pre-publish human review

Before publishing a page, read it aloud and check:

- Could a hundred agencies say the same thing?
- Is there a sentence that sounds impressive but says nothing?
- Does every claim have proof or a clear limit?
- Are several paragraphs built with the same rhythm?
- Are any headings generic labels when they could carry meaning?
- Does the page answer the visitor's likely question without making them search for it?

### The problem

**Heading:** Recognition begins with clarity.  
**Body:** A logo can identify a business, but it cannot decide what the business should mean to customers. Brand strategy makes those decisions explicit. It gives leadership, marketing, sales, and creative teams a shared reference point for what to say, what to prioritise, and what to leave out.

This work is useful when:

- Customers compare you mainly on price.
- Different teams describe the offer in different ways.
- The business has grown beyond its original identity.
- A new product or company is preparing to enter the market.
- Campaigns look active but do not build a recognisable position.
- A rebrand is being discussed before the underlying question is clear.

### What we can help define

#### Audience and market context

Who matters most, what they are trying to solve, how they choose, and what alternatives they already have.

#### Positioning

The useful space the brand can own in the customer's mind, grounded in the offer and the market rather than wishful language.

#### Message system

A practical hierarchy for the main promise, supporting points, proof, objections, tone, and calls to action.

#### Brand architecture

Clear relationships between the parent brand, products, services, programmes, or sub-brands so customers can make sense of the portfolio.

#### Go-to-market direction

The audiences, messages, channels, assets, and sequence needed to bring a new offer or refreshed brand into the market.

### Typical deliverables

- Stakeholder interviews and discovery workshop
- Audience and competitor review
- Positioning framework
- Value proposition and message hierarchy
- Brand story and verbal direction
- Brand architecture recommendations
- Naming support, where required
- Launch or repositioning plan
- Internal brand briefing
- Practical strategy document your team can use

The final scope depends on the decision the business needs to make. We do not include research or documents simply to make the project look larger.

### How the work moves

#### Listen

We review the offer, current materials, customer context, internal views, and previous marketing. Where useful, we speak with customers or other stakeholders.

#### Find the tension

We identify the gap between how the business sees itself and what the market is likely to notice, believe, or value.

#### Make the choices

We develop and test positioning and messaging routes. The goal is not a collection of options. It is a defensible direction.

#### Put it to work

We translate the strategy into a usable system for identity, content, sales, campaigns, websites, and launch activity.

### A good fit

This service is a strong fit when the decision-makers can take part in discovery, the business is willing to make choices, and the team wants a strategy it will actually use. It is not a good fit if the only desired outcome is a longer document that validates a direction already fixed.

### FAQs

#### Do we need brand strategy before a new identity?

If the business is changing position, entering a new market, speaking to a new audience, or struggling to explain its value, strategy should come first. If the direction is already clear and documented, we can review it and move into identity work.

#### Can you work with an existing brand?

Yes. The work may involve sharpening the current position and message rather than replacing everything. A useful strategy should show what to keep as clearly as what to change.

#### Does the process include customer research?

It can. We recommend the level of research based on the decision, access to customers, timing, and budget. We will not present assumptions as research.

#### What happens after the strategy is approved?

We can carry the direction into identity, content, a website, campaign planning, or a launch. You can also take the strategy to an internal team or another specialist. Handover materials will make the next step clear.

## 32. Launch article: Before spending more on ads

**Route:** `/insights/before-spending-more-on-ads`  
**Category:** Performance  
**SEO title:** `What to Fix Before Spending More on Ads | Kreative Sparq`  
**Meta description:** `Before increasing your advertising budget, check the offer, landing page, lead response, tracking, campaign economics, and creative supply.`  
**H1:** Before you spend more on ads, fix the path after the click.  
**Standfirst:** More budget sends more people through the system you already have. If that system is unclear, slow, or difficult to measure, advertising will expose the problem faster.  
**Author:** `{{ARTICLE_AUTHOR}}`  
**Reviewer:** `{{ARTICLE_REVIEWER_OPTIONAL}}`  
**Editorial requirement:** Add at least one approved firsthand or anonymised example before publishing.

Paid advertising is easy to scale in one sense. You can increase the budget in a few clicks. The harder question is whether the business is ready for the extra attention.

When a campaign is underperforming, the ad account is often the first place people look. Sometimes the setup is the problem. But the cause may sit earlier in the offer or later in the customer journey. Before spending more, check the six parts below.

### 1. Can someone understand the offer in one sentence?

An ad has limited time to earn attention. If the offer takes several paragraphs to explain, the campaign starts with a disadvantage.

Write one sentence that answers:

- What are you offering?
- Who is it for?
- What useful change does it provide?
- Why should someone consider it now?

This sentence does not need to be clever. It needs to be understood. Test it with someone close to the intended customer but outside the project team. Ask them to explain it back in their own words. If their answer changes the meaning, keep working.

### 2. Does the landing page continue the same promise?

The ad and landing page are one conversation. A person should not click a specific promise and arrive on a general home page that asks them to search for the relevant information.

Check whether the destination page:

- Repeats the core offer in plain language
- Shows who the offer is for
- Answers the main questions and objections
- Provides enough proof for the decision
- Makes the next action easy to see and complete
- Works properly on a phone and a slower connection

Removing unnecessary choices often helps more than adding another block of persuasive copy.

### 3. Can the business respond when interest arrives?

A lead is not a result if nobody follows up. A purchase is not healthy growth if fulfilment breaks under demand.

Before increasing spend, define:

- Who receives each inquiry
- How quickly that person is expected to respond
- What information they need
- How the lead will be qualified
- Where the outcome will be recorded
- What happens when the owner is unavailable

Test the process yourself. Submit the form on a phone. Check the confirmation. Follow the notification. See how long it takes to reach a person who can act.

### 4. Is the important action being measured?

Clicks and impressions explain delivery. They do not tell you whether the campaign produced useful business action.

Choose the event closest to value that can be measured responsibly. Depending on the business, that may be a completed purchase, qualified inquiry, booking, application, registration, or phone call.

Then check the basics:

- Does the event fire only when the action succeeds?
- Is the same action counted twice by different tools?
- Can the business connect a lead to its source?
- Are test submissions excluded where possible?
- Is consent handled correctly?
- Can the sales or service team record what happened after the initial inquiry?

Attribution will never be perfect. The goal is evidence good enough to support a decision, with the limits understood.

### 5. Do the numbers make commercial sense?

A low cost per click can still produce an expensive customer. A high cost per lead can still be acceptable for a valuable, profitable service. Platform averages cannot answer the business question on their own.

Work backwards from the economics:

1. What is a completed sale or qualified opportunity worth?
2. What proportion of qualified leads usually become customers?
3. What gross margin or contribution is available after delivery costs?
4. How long does it take for the value to arrive?
5. What acquisition cost can the business carry?

If those numbers are unknown, use a documented estimate and label it as an assumption. The first campaign can help replace assumptions with evidence.

### 6. Is there enough creative to learn from?

Running one image and one headline for months is not a testing plan. Producing dozens of random variations is not one either.

Build variations around useful questions:

- Which customer problem deserves the lead?
- Does a demonstration work better than a claim?
- Which proof reduces hesitation?
- Does the audience respond to the outcome, the process, or the offer terms?
- Which format suits the platform and stage of awareness?

Change one meaningful variable at a time where practical. Keep a record of the reason for each variation so the result teaches the team something.

### A quick readiness check

Increase the budget only when you can answer yes to most of these questions:

- The offer is easy to explain.
- The landing page matches the ad.
- The desired action works on mobile.
- The response owner and process are clear.
- Conversion tracking has been tested.
- The business knows what it can afford to acquire a customer or qualified lead.
- There is a plan for creative testing.
- Fulfilment can handle additional demand.

If several answers are no, the next investment may belong in the path rather than the media budget.

### Closing

Advertising does not sit above the business. It runs through the offer, page, people, systems, and economics. Fixing those parts gives the campaign a fairer test and gives the team better information about what to do next.

## 33. Launch article: How to brief a marketing agency

**Route:** `/insights/how-to-brief-a-marketing-agency`  
**Category:** Running Marketing  
**SEO title:** `How to Brief a Marketing Agency | Kreative Sparq`  
**Meta description:** `Write a useful marketing agency brief without a long document. Cover the business context, audience, desired change, constraints, evidence, timing, and decisions.`  
**H1:** How to brief a marketing agency without writing 30 pages.  
**Standfirst:** A useful brief does not need polished marketing language. It needs enough context for the agency to understand the decision, the constraints, and what a good outcome would change.  
**Author:** `{{ARTICLE_AUTHOR}}`  
**Editorial requirement:** Add one approved example of a weak brief rewritten into a useful brief before publishing.

Some briefs are too thin to guide the work. Others are so detailed that they leave no room to diagnose the problem. The best briefs sit between those extremes.

They tell the agency what the business knows, what it assumes, what has already been decided, and where help is needed. The eight sections below are enough for most first conversations.

### 1. Start with the business situation

Explain what is happening now and why the project matters at this moment.

Useful context might include:

- The company is entering a new market.
- A product is launching on a fixed date.
- Sales have grown, but the brand no longer reflects the business.
- Marketing activity has increased without enough qualified inquiries.
- Several teams are communicating the same offer differently.

Avoid beginning with a deliverable if you are not sure it is the answer. “We need 20 social posts” tells the agency what to make. “Our target customers do not understand that we now serve businesses, not only individuals” explains the problem those posts would need to solve.

### 2. Describe the audience as people making a decision

Demographics may be relevant, but they are rarely enough. Explain the situation the audience is in.

- What are they trying to accomplish?
- What triggers them to look for help?
- What alternatives do they consider?
- What makes them hesitate?
- Who influences or approves the decision?
- Where do they already look for information?

If the answers are uncertain, say so. Research can be part of the assignment.

### 3. State what needs to change

Good objectives describe a change in understanding, behaviour, or business performance. They do not simply restate the activity.

Compare these two lines:

> We need a product launch campaign across social media.

> We need existing customers to understand why the new product is different and join the waitlist before the November launch.

The second version gives the agency something to solve and eventually measure.

### 4. Share what has already been tried

Previous work saves time, even when it failed.

Include relevant campaigns, research, customer feedback, landing pages, sales material, analytics, content, or internal proposals. Explain what the team believes worked, what did not, and how confident it is in that interpretation.

Do not hide a previous failure to make the new project feel like a clean start. The failure may contain the most useful evidence in the brief.

### 5. Separate fixed decisions from open questions

Make three short lists:

#### Fixed

Decisions or constraints that cannot change, such as a regulatory requirement, launch date, product name, approved market, or signed partnership.

#### Preferred

Directions the team likes but can reconsider if the agency has a stronger reason.

#### Open

Questions the agency is expected to help answer.

This distinction prevents time being wasted on impossible options while protecting room for better thinking.

### 6. Be direct about budget

A budget is part of the design problem. It affects research, production, channels, talent, media, timing, geography, and how much can be tested.

If the exact amount is not approved, give a realistic range or explain the approval process. An agency can then propose a scope that fits the decision. Hiding the budget often produces proposals that cannot be compared because each supplier has imagined a different assignment.

Separate agency fees, production, media spend, printing, venues, travel, talent, technology, and other third-party costs where possible.

### 7. Explain the timing and the reason behind it

Give the desired start date, fixed milestones, launch date, internal review windows, procurement steps, and external dependencies.

If a date is flexible, say so. If it is fixed, explain why. A public event date and an internal preference do not carry the same risk.

### 8. Name the decision-makers

The agency needs to know who provides input, who consolidates feedback, who approves the work, and who signs the commercial agreement.

This does not require a complicated governance chart. Role names and a simple approval path are enough at the start.

### A brief you can copy

Use this structure:

1. **Business situation:** What is happening, and why now?
2. **Audience:** Who needs to understand, believe, or do something?
3. **Desired change:** What should be different after the work?
4. **Current evidence:** What do we know, and where did it come from?
5. **Previous attempts:** What has already been tried?
6. **Scope clues:** What work may be needed, without treating it as final?
7. **Constraints:** Budget, date, approvals, legal, technical, and operational limits.
8. **Decision process:** Who gives input and who approves?
9. **Open questions:** What do we need the agency to help decide?

The brief can be one or two pages. Links and appendices can carry supporting detail.

### Closing

A good brief does not remove every unknown. It puts the right unknowns on the table. That gives the agency a fair chance to diagnose the assignment, propose the right scope, and explain the trade-offs before production begins.

## 34. Launch article: Activity is not a content strategy

**Route:** `/insights/social-media-activity-is-not-a-content-strategy`  
**Category:** Content & Social  
**SEO title:** `Social Media Activity Is Not a Content Strategy | Kreative Sparq`  
**Meta description:** `Learn the difference between a posting calendar and a content strategy built around audience needs, channel roles, production, distribution, and measurement.`  
**H1:** Posting regularly is not the same as having a content strategy.  
**Standfirst:** A calendar tells you when something will be published. A strategy explains why the content should exist, who it is for, and what it should help happen next.  
**Author:** `{{ARTICLE_AUTHOR}}`  
**Editorial requirement:** Add an approved example calendar or anonymised content-system example before publishing.

A brand can post every day and still say very little. The feed may look active, but the audience cannot explain what the business is known for. Production stays urgent. Results are reported one post at a time. The team keeps asking what to publish next.

That is a content schedule without a content strategy.

The strategy does not have to be long. It needs to make a small set of decisions that help the team choose, create, distribute, and judge content consistently.

### Give each channel a job

Different platforms can reach different behaviours. The same person may use LinkedIn to follow industry thinking, Instagram to judge visual credibility, YouTube to learn a process, and email to keep track of an offer.

For every channel, finish this sentence:

> We use this channel to help {{AUDIENCE}} {{ACTION_OR_UNDERSTANDING}} by publishing {{CONTENT_TYPE}}.

If two channels have the same answer, that may be fine. But copying every post everywhere without considering format or intent is a distribution shortcut, not a strategy.

### Build themes from customer questions

Content themes should come from the business, offer, and audience. They are not broad words such as “motivation,” “education,” or “engagement.”

A useful theme has boundaries. For example:

- Decisions customers should make before buying
- Common mistakes that increase cost or delay
- How the service works behind the scenes
- Evidence that shows the difference between weak and strong execution
- Questions the sales or support team answers repeatedly
- Changes in the market that affect the customer's next move

Each theme should support the position the brand wants to own. If a topic earns attention but trains people to associate the brand with the wrong thing, it may not belong in the plan.

### Match formats to the idea

Do not begin with “we need more reels” or “carousels are performing.” Start with what the audience needs to see or understand.

- A demonstration may need video.
- A comparison may work as a table or carousel.
- A detailed answer may deserve an article or longer video.
- A timely observation may need a short text post.
- A customer story may need several formats across the buying path.

Format follows the communication job. Platform behaviour still matters, but it should shape the expression rather than decide the idea by itself.

### Design a production system the team can sustain

A strategy that depends on constant emergency work will not survive a busy month.

Define:

- Who supplies subject knowledge
- Who writes, designs, records, and edits
- Which work can be produced in batches
- What requires live or timely input
- Who checks facts and brand accuracy
- Who approves the final content
- How long each step is allowed to take
- Where files, feedback, and final versions are kept

The publishing rhythm should reflect available capacity. Three strong, connected pieces may be more useful than seven rushed posts that leave no time for response or learning.

### Plan distribution, not only publication

Publishing is the start of distribution.

Ask how each piece will travel:

- Will the founder, team, or partners share it?
- Does it support a sales conversation?
- Can it answer a question in email or WhatsApp?
- Should it be adapted for another channel?
- Is paid distribution appropriate?
- Can a strong response become the next piece of content?
- Does the website hold a deeper version that search visitors can find later?

This approach lets one useful idea do more work without repeating the exact same asset everywhere.

### Decide how the brand will respond

Community management is part of the content system. Comments and direct messages show where the message is clear, where questions remain, and what the audience cares about enough to say publicly.

Create simple rules for:

- Routine questions
- Sales inquiries
- Complaints
- Sensitive or legal issues
- Misinformation
- Spam and abuse
- Response hours and escalation

The person publishing content should know where their responsibility ends and who owns the next step.

### Measure the role of the content

Not every post should be judged by sales. Not every post should escape judgment because it was created for “awareness.”

Assign the intended role before publishing:

- **Reach:** Did the right people encounter the idea?
- **Attention:** Did they spend enough time to understand it?
- **Response:** Did they save, share, reply, or ask a useful question?
- **Action:** Did they visit, subscribe, inquire, register, or buy?
- **Learning:** Did the content reveal a message, objection, or format worth developing?

Look for patterns across a meaningful group of content. One unusually high or low post should not rewrite the strategy by itself.

### A 30-minute content strategy exercise

Write one page with these headings:

1. The audience we need to reach
2. The position we want the content to build
3. The questions we can answer better than most
4. The job of each active channel
5. The formats we can produce well
6. The publishing rhythm we can sustain
7. The actions we want to make easier
8. The numbers and feedback we will review

Then compare the next month's calendar against the page. Remove content that has no clear audience, role, or connection to the brand.

### Closing
